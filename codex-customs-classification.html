The plain version
  • Every traded product gets an HS code — a 6-digit international classification, extended to 8–10 digits nationally — that determines the duty rate, licensing requirements, and which trade agreements apply. Get the code wrong and everything built on top of it is wrong too.
  • Classification follows the General Rules of Interpretation (GRI), a strict hierarchical order — you cannot skip to a specific rule because it gives a more convenient answer; each rule only applies once the ones before it fail to resolve the question.
  • Rules of origin determine whether a product actually qualifies for preferential treatment under a trade agreement — usually through a regional value content calculation or a required "tariff shift" in the non-originating components.
  • On 20 February 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs — invalidating a large share of tariffs collected since 2025 and reshaping the entire near-term US tariff landscape. This is not settled; new tariff actions under different legal authorities followed within days.
Classification

The General Rules of Interpretation — a ladder, not a menu.

More than 200 countries use the Harmonized System as the basis for their tariff codes. The first six digits are identical everywhere in the world; countries then extend the code for their own national tariff schedule — a 10-digit HTS number for US imports, an 8–10 digit Schedule B for US exports, the EU's Combined Nomenclature (CN) and TARIC system for EU trade. The GRI is the legal method every classifier — human or otherwise — must apply, strictly in order:

1

GRI 1 — classification is determined by the heading and section notes. If the wording clearly fits, the job is done here.

2

GRI 2 — covers incomplete or unassembled goods, and mixtures, extending headings to unfinished versions of a classified product.

3

GRI 3 — for goods that could fit multiple headings: the more specific description wins; failing that, classify by essential character; failing that, use the heading that appears last in numerical order among the valid options.

4

GRI 4 — if nothing fits perfectly, classify as whatever the goods most resemble. Used rarely, for genuine edge cases.

5

GRI 5 — governs packaging: some containers are classified with the product inside them, others aren't.

6

GRI 6 — once the heading (4-digit) is settled, the same GRI logic repeats to choose between subheadings (6-digit) and national tariff lines (8–10 digit).

Verdict

Classification depends on a product's composition, function, and intended use — not its marketing description, and not what a foreign supplier's local paperwork already says. Using a supplier-provided code without independent verification is one of the most common and most avoidable classification errors.

Who's actually on the hook

The importer of record — not the broker.

Under the reasonable-care standard established by the Customs Modernization Act of 1993, the legal burden for correct classification sits with the importer of record, not the customs broker who files the entry. Hiring a broker doesn't transfer that responsibility — if the classification is wrong, the importer is the one exposed, regardless of who typed the code into the system.

This matters more than it sounds: statistical estimates put classification errors at roughly 30–40% of customs compliance violations across the EU and US, and correction periods can reach back 3–5 years, exposing a company to retroactive liability for duties, penalties, or both on shipments it thought were long settled.

Certificates of origin

Proving where a product actually came from — not where it shipped from.

A certificate of origin makes a legal claim: that a product qualifies as "originating" under a specific trade agreement, which usually unlocks a preferential duty rate. Two common tests determine this:

  • Regional value content (RVC) — a required percentage of the product's value must come from qualifying countries. Automotive parts under USMCA, for instance, generally need 75% regional value content.
  • Tariff shift — non-originating components must be classified under a different HS chapter, heading, or subheading than the finished product, showing they were substantively transformed within the trade area. A cookie made with non-originating flour (Chapter 11) qualifies, because the flour sits outside Chapter 19 where the finished cookie is classified; a cookie made from a non-originating baking mix does not, because mixes are already classified in Chapter 19 — the same chapter as the finished product.

Under USMCA specifically, there's no single fixed certificate form — a free-format certification is allowed, provided it contains nine required data elements, and it can be prepared by the importer, exporter, or producer. The importer must retain that documentation for five years.

Verdict

An unverified or informally-assumed claim of origin is exactly the kind of gap that surfaces years later in an audit, not at the border — retain the underlying support for every certificate you issue or rely on, not just the certificate itself.

The 2026 shift

The Supreme Court just removed a major tariff tool — but not the underlying uncertainty.

Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump — decided 20 February 2026

In a 6–3 ruling, the U.S. Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. The decision invalidated both the targeted "fentanyl" tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China, and the broader global "reciprocal" tariffs — potentially unlocking tens of billions of dollars in refunds for importers who paid duties under that authority since 2025.

The ruling did not end tariff activity. Within days, new tariffs were proclaimed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a statute permitting temporary tariffs (capped at 150 days without congressional extension) to address balance-of-payments concerns — starting at 10% and rising toward 15%. Separately, Section 301 investigations targeting China, Vietnam, Taiwan, Mexico, Japan, the EU, and others were launched in March 2026, a slower but potentially more durable route to country- or product-specific tariffs. The Court's ruling addressed only IEEPA; it said nothing about Section 232 or Section 301 authority, both of which remain fully available.

Separately and consequentially, the U.S. eliminated the $800 de minimis exemption globally as of 24 February 2026. Every import shipment, regardless of value, now requires a formal customs entry with HTS classification and verified country of origin — a shift that pulled millions of small parcel shipments that previously cleared automatically into the same classification and origin-verification requirements as commercial freight.

Verdict

Don't read the IEEPA ruling as tariff relief — read it as a change in which legal mechanism is being used to impose tariffs, with real litigation still underway over the replacement mechanisms themselves. Refund eligibility, if you paid IEEPA-based duties, is a live and separate process worth checking regardless of what happens next.
