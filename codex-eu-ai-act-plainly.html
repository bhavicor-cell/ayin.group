The EU AI Act,
Plainly.

Chapter I

General Provisions

Art. 1Subject Matter

This law exists to make AI safer for people while still letting the AI industry grow. It protects health, safety, fundamental rights, democracy, the rule of law, and the environment from AI-related harm — without shutting down innovation.

To do that, it sets out: rules for placing AI on the market and using it; a list of banned AI practices; specific rules for high-risk AI systems and the people who build or use them; transparency rules for certain AI systems; rules for general-purpose AI models; a system of market monitoring and enforcement; and support measures for smaller companies and startups.

Art. 2Scope

This law reaches further than most people expect. It applies to:

  • Anyone who builds and places an AI system on the EU market, or puts one into service in the EU — whether that company is based in the EU or not. Being based outside the EU does not exempt you.
  • Anyone who uses ("deploys") an AI system and is based or located in the EU.
  • Anyone — even outside the EU — whose AI system's output is used inside the EU.
  • Importers and distributors of AI systems.
  • Manufacturers who sell a physical product with an embedded AI system under their own brand.
  • EU-based legal representatives appointed by non-EU providers.
  • People located in the EU who are affected by an AI system, regardless of nationality or residency — physical presence in the EU at the time of impact is what triggers protection.

Some carve-outs exist. High-risk systems that are safety components of products already regulated by other EU product laws are only subject to a narrow slice of this Act. This Act doesn't apply to pure national security, defence, or military use; to non-EU public authorities or international organizations acting under law-enforcement cooperation agreements (provided fundamental rights are safeguarded); to existing liability rules for intermediary services; or to AI used purely for scientific research — though once something built for research is placed on the market, the exemption ends.

Purely personal, non-professional use by individuals is outside scope. Open-source AI released for free, with full transparency, is largely exempt — unless it's classified high-risk or falls under the transparency or systemic-risk rules.

Art. 3Definitions — selected key terms

  • AI system — a machine-based system that operates with some independence, may keep adapting after deployment, and generates outputs — predictions, content, recommendations, decisions — that can affect real or virtual environments, based on the goals it's given.
  • Provider — whoever builds an AI system (or has it built) and places it on the market or puts it into service under their own name or brand, whether they charge for it or not.
  • Deployer — whoever uses an AI system under their own authority, except for purely personal, non-professional use.
  • Placing on the market — the first time a system becomes available on the EU market. Putting into service — supplying it for its first real use.
  • High-risk AI system — defined in Chapter III: broadly, systems used in employment, education, law enforcement, migration, credit scoring, and critical infrastructure, where a wrong decision seriously harms people.
  • General-purpose AI model — a model, often trained on huge amounts of data, capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks and buildable into many downstream products — think large language models.
  • Systemic risk — a risk tied to a general-purpose AI model's very high capability or wide reach, the kind that could cascade across the whole EU market or society.
  • Deep fake — AI-generated or altered image, audio, or video that looks like a real person, object, place, or event but isn't authentic.

Art. 4AI Literacy

Providers and deployers must take reasonable steps to make sure their own staff — and anyone else operating the AI system on their behalf — actually understand it: how it works, what its limits are, and who it affects.

Chapter II

Prohibited AI Practices

Art. 5Banned AI Practices

Eight categories of AI use are flatly prohibited, regardless of who's doing it or why:

  • Subliminal or manipulative AI that distorts a person's behavior below their conscious awareness, likely to cause significant harm.
  • Exploiting vulnerabilities — age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation — to distort someone's behavior in a way likely to cause significant harm.
  • Social scoring — evaluating or ranking people based on behavior or characteristics, then treating them worse in unrelated contexts, or disproportionately to what they actually did.
  • Predictive policing based purely on profiling — assessing someone's risk of committing a crime based solely on personality traits, with no objective facts tying them to actual criminal activity.
  • Untargeted scraping of faces from the internet or CCTV footage to build or expand facial recognition databases.
  • Emotion recognition in workplaces and schools — banned except for genuine medical or safety reasons.
  • Biometric categorization to infer protected traits — race, political opinions, religion, sexual orientation, and similar — from biometric data.
  • Real-time remote facial recognition in public spaces by police — banned, with narrow exceptions: finding specific crime victims, preventing an imminent terrorist threat, or locating a suspect in a serious listed crime. Even then, it requires prior judicial or independent authorization, strict time and location limits, and a mandatory fundamental-rights impact assessment. No legal decision can be based on the identification alone.
Chapter III

High-Risk AI Systems

Art. 6Classification Rules

An AI system counts as high-risk in two situations: first, if it's a safety component of a product (or is itself a product) already covered by EU product safety law — machinery, toys, medical devices, cars — that legally requires third-party safety certification. Second, if it falls into one of the use-case categories in Annex III (employment, education, law enforcement, and so on).

There's an important carve-out even within Annex III: a system isn't high-risk if it only performs a narrow procedural task, merely improves the result of work a human already finished, checks for deviations from an established human pattern without replacing judgment, or performs a preparatory step before a real decision. But this carve-out disappears entirely if the system profiles individuals — profiling always makes it high-risk.

If a provider decides their Annex III system isn't actually high-risk, they still have to document that reasoning and register it — regulators can ask to see the assessment.

Art. 7Updating the High-Risk List

The European Commission can add or remove use-cases from the high-risk list based on: how the system is used, how many people it affects, how severe the potential harm is, how easily a person could opt out, and whether existing law already protects people adequately.

Art. 8General Compliance

High-risk systems must meet every requirement in this section, judged against their intended purpose and the current state of the art.

Art. 9Risk Management System

Every high-risk AI system needs an ongoing risk management process across its whole lifecycle: identify foreseeable risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights; estimate and evaluate them, including realistic misuse; roll in learnings from real-world monitoring; take targeted action on what's found. Testing must confirm consistent performance before market entry, and providers must specifically consider whether the system could adversely affect anyone under 18 or other vulnerable groups.

Art. 10Data and Data Governance

Training, validation, and testing data must be managed deliberately: how it was collected, why, how it was cleaned and labeled, whether it's representative of the people the system will affect. Providers must actively check for bias that could harm health and safety or lead to unlawful discrimination, and fix what they find. In tightly controlled circumstances, providers may process sensitive personal data specifically to detect and correct bias, subject to strict safeguards.

Art. 11Technical Documentation

Before a high-risk system goes to market, the provider must produce full technical documentation proving it meets every requirement — detailed enough for regulators to assess compliance. Small companies and startups can use a simplified template.

Art. 12Record-Keeping

High-risk systems must automatically log events throughout their operation, so behavior can be traced later — for spotting problems, supporting investigations, and post-market monitoring.

Art. 13Transparency Toward Deployers

The system must be transparent enough that deployers can understand its output and use it properly — clear instructions covering purpose, accuracy and robustness, known risks, how to interpret results, and built-in human oversight measures.

Art. 14Human Oversight

High-risk systems must be built so real people can actually oversee them — understanding limits, watching for blind over-reliance, correctly interpreting output, able to override or stop it entirely. For remote biometric identification specifically, no action can be taken on a match unless at least two qualified people independently verify it.

Art. 15Accuracy, Robustness, and Cybersecurity

High-risk systems need to perform reliably throughout their life, resist errors and unexpected situations, and be secure against attacks that try to manipulate training data, poison the model, or exploit technical vulnerabilities. Expected accuracy levels must be stated clearly in the instructions for use.

Art. 16Provider Obligations

Providers of high-risk systems must: ensure compliance with all requirements above; label the system with name and contact details; run a quality management system; keep required documentation and logs; complete the proper conformity assessment before market entry; issue a formal EU declaration of conformity; affix CE marking; register the system in the EU database; take corrective action if problems surface; meet accessibility standards.

Art. 17Quality Management System

The internal operating system for compliance — regulatory strategy, design and development control, testing procedures, data management, risk management, post-market monitoring, incident reporting, and clear internal accountability. Scales to the size of the provider.

Art. 18–20Documentation, Logs, and Corrective Action

Providers must keep technical documentation and quality-system records for ten years after market entry. Automatically generated logs must be kept for at least six months. If a provider believes their system isn't compliant, they must immediately fix, withdraw, or recall it — and tell distributors, deployers, importers, and their authorized representative.

Art. 21–22Cooperating with Authorities; Authorised Representatives

Providers must hand over documentation to regulators on request, in a language they understand, including access to system logs. Non-EU providers must appoint an EU-based authorized representative before their product reaches the EU market.

Art. 23–24Importers and Distributors

Importers must verify the system went through proper conformity assessment, has full documentation, bears CE marking, and has an EU authorized representative before placing it on the market — and hold it back if they suspect non-compliance or counterfeiting. Distributors carry a similar verification duty.

Art. 25Responsibility Along the Value Chain

If a distributor, importer, or third party puts their own brand on someone else's high-risk system, substantially modifies it, or repurposes a non-high-risk system into a high-risk one — they become the "provider" for legal purposes and inherit all provider obligations. The original provider must still cooperate and share necessary information.

Art. 26Deployer Obligations

Deployers must use the system per its instructions, assign human oversight to genuinely competent people, monitor operation, and suspend use immediately if risk is suspected — informing the provider and relevant authority. Employers must tell workers and representatives before deploying a high-risk system at the workplace. Public-sector deployers of Annex III systems must register their use in the EU database before using it.

Real-time or after-the-fact biometric identification by law enforcement carries extra rules: post-event biometric ID searches generally need judicial or independent authorization, are limited to specific criminal investigations, and can never be used as an untargeted surveillance tool.

Art. 27Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment

Before deploying certain high-risk systems, public bodies and specific private entities (banks, insurers) must assess the impact on fundamental rights first — who's affected, what could go wrong, what will be done if it does. Results must be reported to the relevant regulator.

Art. 28–39Notified Bodies

Each EU country must set up an authority responsible for vetting and monitoring the independent bodies ("notified bodies") that certify high-risk AI systems — genuinely independent, technically competent, properly resourced. Countries share information about notified bodies with the Commission and each other.

Art. 40–42Standards and Presumption of Conformity

If a provider follows official EU-harmonized technical standards, their system is presumed to meet the legal requirements those standards cover. Where no standard exists yet, the Commission can issue "common specifications" as a stopgap.

Art. 43Conformity Assessment

Most high-risk systems can be self-certified by the provider through internal checks. Biometric systems generally require independent third-party assessment. Any substantial modification later triggers a fresh assessment.

Art. 44–49Certificates, Declarations, CE Marking, Registration

Certificates from notified bodies are valid for four to five years depending on system type, and can be suspended or withdrawn if the system stops complying. Providers must issue a written EU declaration of conformity and affix CE marking before market entry. Except for a narrow set of law-enforcement and migration systems (registered in a secured, non-public part of the database), high-risk systems must be registered in a public EU database before reaching the market.

Chapter IV

Transparency Obligations

Art. 50Transparency for Certain AI Systems

Even AI that isn't high-risk carries disclosure duties:

  • If a person is interacting with an AI system (like a chatbot), they must be told, unless it's obvious from context.
  • AI-generated audio, image, video, or text must be marked in a machine-readable way as artificially generated — with narrow exceptions for minor editing assistance.
  • People exposed to emotion-recognition or biometric-categorization systems must be informed.
  • Deep fakes must be labeled as artificially generated or manipulated, unless clearly satire, art, or fiction — a lighter disclosure suffices there. AI-generated text published on matters of public interest must also be disclosed, unless a human editor takes responsibility for it.
Chapter V

General-Purpose AI Models

Art. 51Classifying Systemic Risk

A general-purpose AI model carries "systemic risk" if it has high-impact capabilities — presumed once training computation exceeds 10²⁵ floating-point operations — or if the Commission decides, case by case, that its capability or reach is equivalent.

Art. 52Notification Procedure

Providers whose model crosses that threshold must notify the Commission within two weeks. They can argue their specific model is an exception, but the Commission can reject that and classify it as systemic-risk anyway.

Art. 53Obligations for All General-Purpose AI Model Providers

Every provider must: keep up-to-date technical documentation of the model, including training and evaluation; give downstream developers enough information to use it safely and meet their own obligations; maintain a policy for complying with EU copyright law, including honoring rights-holders' opt-outs from data mining; and publish a sufficiently detailed public summary of training content. Providers releasing models under genuinely open free licenses are exempt from some duties — except copyright compliance — unless the model carries systemic risk.

Art. 54Authorised Representatives

Non-EU providers of general-purpose AI models must appoint an EU-based representative before entering the EU market, similarly to Article 22.

Art. 55Extra Obligations for Systemic-Risk Models

Providers of systemic-risk models must additionally: run standardized model evaluations, including adversarial ("red-team") testing; actively assess and mitigate systemic risks at EU level; track and report serious incidents to the Commission; maintain strong cybersecurity for the model and its infrastructure.

Art. 56Codes of Practice

Until formal harmonized standards exist, the EU AI Office will facilitate voluntary codes of practice that providers can rely on to demonstrate compliance with the general-purpose AI model rules.

Chapter VI

Measures Supporting Innovation

Art. 57–61Regulatory Sandboxes and Real-World Testing

Every EU country must run at least one AI regulatory sandbox — a supervised space to develop and test AI systems under regulatory guidance before full market launch, with legal certainty and reduced compliance friction. Complying in good faith with sandbox guidance shields participants from fines. High-risk systems can also be tested in real-world conditions outside a sandbox, under strict conditions: a registered testing plan, informed consent from test subjects, time limits (generally six months, extendable), and the ability to fully reverse the system's outputs.

Art. 62–63Support for SMEs; Simplified Compliance

Small businesses and startups get priority sandbox access, reduced conformity-assessment fees, dedicated guidance channels, and standardized templates. Microenterprises can meet the quality-management-system requirement in a simplified form.

Chapter VII

Governance

Art. 64–69The AI Office, the Board, and Expert Bodies

The European Commission runs this system through the AI Office, which builds EU-wide expertise and directly supervises general-purpose AI models. A European Artificial Intelligence Board, made up of one representative per country, coordinates enforcement and advises the Commission. An advisory forum of industry, civil society, and academic stakeholders feeds in outside expertise, and a scientific panel of independent experts supports technical enforcement, including flagging models that may pose systemic risk.

Art. 70National Competent Authorities

Each country must designate its own notifying authority and market surveillance authority, give them proper funding and technically qualified staff, and publish how to contact them.

Chapter VIII

EU Database

Art. 71The EU Database

The Commission maintains a public, searchable database of registered high-risk AI systems. Providers and — for public-sector use — deployers must submit their registration data before market entry or use. A narrow set of law-enforcement and migration-related systems is kept in a secure, non-public section.

Chapter IX

Monitoring, Information Sharing, Enforcement

Art. 72Post-Market Monitoring

Providers must actively collect and analyze real-world performance data on their high-risk systems throughout their lifetime, to catch problems and confirm continued compliance.

Art. 73Reporting Serious Incidents

Serious incidents — death, serious harm to health, serious disruption of critical infrastructure, breaches of fundamental-rights law, serious property or environmental damage — must be reported to the relevant authority, generally within 15 days, shortened to 2 days for widespread infringements and 10 days if someone died.

Art. 74–84Market Surveillance Powers

National market surveillance authorities can demand documentation, access training data and even source code under specific conditions, run remote or on-site inspections, and order corrective action, withdrawal, or recall of non-compliant systems. Special surveillance rules apply to systems tied to financial services, law enforcement, and general-purpose AI models built and deployed by the same provider (which fall under the AI Office directly). A formal EU-level safeguard procedure resolves disagreements between countries or with the Commission over whether a national enforcement action was justified.

Art. 85–87Remedies

Any person can file a complaint with a market surveillance authority over a suspected breach of this law. Anyone affected by certain high-risk AI decisions has a right to a clear explanation of the AI system's role. Whistleblower protections under existing EU law extend to people reporting breaches of this Act.

Art. 88–94Enforcement Against General-Purpose AI Model Providers

The Commission — acting through the AI Office — has exclusive power to enforce the general-purpose AI model rules. It can demand documentation, run its own evaluations (including with independent experts), and require providers to take mitigation measures, restrict market availability, or withdraw a model entirely.

Chapter X

Codes of Conduct and Guidelines

Art. 95–96Voluntary Codes and Commission Guidance

Providers of AI systems that aren't high-risk are encouraged — not required — to voluntarily adopt some of the same safeguards, through codes of conduct addressing things like environmental sustainability, AI literacy, and inclusive design. The Commission will also issue practical guidance on applying this Regulation, paying particular attention to the needs of SMEs and startups.

Chapter XI

Delegated Powers

Art. 97–98

The Commission can update technical details of this Regulation — like the high-risk list, technical documentation requirements, or systemic-risk thresholds — through delegated acts, subject to five-year renewable authorization and oversight by the European Parliament and Council.

Chapter XII

Penalties

Art. 99Fines

  • Breaching the banned-practices list (Article 5) — up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.
  • Breaching most other provider, importer, distributor, or deployer obligations — up to €15 million or 3% of turnover.
  • Giving false or misleading information to authorities — up to €7.5 million or 1% of turnover.

Smaller companies get the lower of the two figures in each case, and regulators must weigh proportionality, intent, and cooperation when setting the actual amount.

Art. 100Fines on EU Institutions

The European Data Protection Supervisor can fine EU institutions themselves for breaches — up to €1.5 million for banned practices, €750,000 for other breaches.

Art. 101Fines for General-Purpose AI Model Providers

The Commission can fine general-purpose AI model providers up to 3% of global turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher, for breaching the model-specific rules or obstructing the Commission's evaluations.

Chapter XIII

Final Provisions

Art. 102–110Amendments to Other EU Laws

A series of technical amendments align this Regulation with existing EU product-safety laws covering aviation, vehicles, machinery, marine equipment, and rail — folding AI-specific safety requirements into those existing frameworks rather than creating conflicting parallel rules.

Art. 111Transition for Existing Systems

AI systems already on the market get transition time: large-scale government IT systems until the end of 2030; other high-risk systems only need to comply if significantly redesigned after August 2026 (public-sector high-risk systems get until 2030 regardless); and general-purpose AI models already on the market before August 2025 have until August 2027 to fully comply.

Art. 112Review

The Commission will keep reviewing this Regulation — assessing annually whether the high-risk and banned-practice lists need updating, and running a fuller review roughly every four years, including whether a dedicated EU AI agency is eventually needed.

Art. 113Entry Into Force

The Regulation entered into force twenty days after publication. Most of it applies from 2 August 2026. But the general provisions and banned practices (Chapters I–II) applied earlier, from 2 February 2025; the governance structure, general-purpose AI model rules, and penalty framework applied from 2 August 2025; and the high-risk product-safety integration rules (Article 6(1)) apply last, from 2 August 2027.
