Three findings
I What it is
Intent

Whether compliance was something your business actually did, or something it says it believed in. Regulators don't take your word for it — they read it. Dated policies, recorded decisions, systems that were running before anything went wrong: that's what intent looks like on paper.

What it means for you

If the record exists, the conversation starts from "this business tried." If it doesn't, it starts from "this business didn't care" — and everything after is uphill.

Intent is read, never heard
II What it is
Reasonable Steps

The standard you're measured against isn't your effort or your intentions — it's what a careful business in your position would have done. That's an objective test, and it's answered with evidence: what existed, when it existed, who was responsible for it.

What it means for you

This is the test most businesses fail not because they did nothing, but because they can't show what they did. An unwritten process and no process are the same thing in a hearing room.

Measured against the standard — not your story
III What it is
Willful
Ignorance

The finding that you knew, or should have known, and didn't act. It doesn't require malice — inaction in the presence of knowledge is enough. And here is the part that surprises people: once you've been warned, every day without action builds the case.

What it means for you

This finding strips your defences. Warnings become proceedings; an incident becomes an example. It is the one outcome infrastructure exists to make impossible — because a maintained record is, by definition, the opposite of ignoring.

The finding that removes every protection

Can you
prove it?

01

That you intended to comply

Not believed in compliance — did it. Decisions recorded, policies dated, responsibility assigned. Belief has no paper trail; intent does.

02

That you took reasonable steps

Measured against what a careful business in your position would have done — and answered only with evidence of what existed, and when.

03

That you were not negligent

The absence of a record is read as the absence of care. Negligence is rarely proven — it is presumed, and it is yours to rebut.
