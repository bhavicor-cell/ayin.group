The Line Between a
Claim and a Cure.

Structure-function claims are allowed without pre-approval. Disease claims aren't. Drawing that line correctly is the entire skill — and the FDA is actively enforcing it in 2026.

JurisdictionUnited States (FDA-enforced)
VerticalDietary supplements & nutraceuticals
StageClear
Last reviewed15 Jul 2026
In thirty seconds
The plain version
  • A supplement can claim it supports a body structure or function ("supports immune health") without FDA pre-approval — but the moment a claim says it will treat, cure, or prevent a disease, the product is legally a drug, regardless of what it's actually made of.
  • Every structure-function claim needs the manufacturer to hold substantiation that it's truthful and not misleading, and requires a notification to FDA within 30 days of first marketing the claim — a procedural filing, not an approval.
  • New dietary ingredients — anything not marketed in the US before 15 October 1994 — require a separate NDI notification under 21 U.S.C. 350b before the product can be sold.
  • 2026 has brought a real, ongoing wave of FDA enforcement against supplement marketing found on websites, social media, and even podcast interviews — the agency is actively reading promotional content, not just labels.
The line, drawn plainly

What separates a supplement claim from a drug claim.

Under DSHEA, the dividing line isn't about what's in the bottle — it's about what you say the bottle does. A product marketed to supplement the diet with nutrients, making only structure-function claims, is a supplement. A product marketed to treat, cure, or prevent a disease is a drug — regardless of its physical form or actual ingredients.

Allowed — structure/function
  • "Supports healthy immune function"
  • "Helps maintain normal blood sugar levels already within a healthy range"
  • "Promotes joint flexibility"
  • General well-being claims, and classical nutrient-deficiency-disease claims (with required disease-prevalence context)
Not allowed — disease claims
  • "Treats" or "cures" any named disease
  • "Prevents" diabetes, cancer, Alzheimer's, depression, or infertility — all categories FDA has specifically issued warning letters over
  • Implying FDA approval, review, or evaluation the product never received
  • A "generic" framing implying equivalence to an approved drug
Verdict

FDA's enforcement lens looks at the "net impression" of your marketing as a whole — not just individual sentences in isolation. Even technically accurate statements can render a product misbranded if the overall presentation leads a reasonable consumer to believe it treats a disease.

The notification requirement

Not pre-approval — but a real, missable deadline.

FDA does not pre-approve structure-function claims. But the manufacturer must have substantiation that the claim is truthful and not misleading, and must submit a notification with the exact text of the claim to FDA no later than 30 days after first marketing the product with that claim. The notification must include the company's name and address, the exact claim text, the product name, and a statement that the claim is made under 21 U.S.C. §343(r)(6). FDA provides an ePortal for these submissions.

Every structure-function claim must also carry the DSHEA disclaimer: "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease." As of late 2025, FDA is exercising enforcement discretion on exactly where that disclaimer has to physically appear — the regulation technically requires it on every panel bearing a claim, but FDA is considering loosening that placement rule and, in the meantime, isn't enforcing the per-panel requirement while it decides. It is still enforcing the requirement to include the disclaimer on the label at all, and to link it clearly to each claim.

Verdict

Don't read the current placement leniency as the disclaimer becoming optional — it isn't. What's temporarily unenforced is only the strict per-panel repetition rule, and that could be formally changed or reversed as FDA's review concludes.

New Dietary Ingredients

The gate that catches ingredient innovation.

Any dietary ingredient not marketed in the US before 15 October 1994 is a "new dietary ingredient" (NDI), and manufacturers must submit an NDI notification under 21 U.S.C. 350b providing FDA with information supporting a reasonable expectation of safety, before the product reaches market. This is the gate most likely to catch companies innovating with newer or less-established ingredients — a product can be otherwise perfectly labeled and still be adulterated if it contains an NDI for which FDA never received adequate safety information.

FDA has also used a narrower, ingredient-specific tool: enforcement discretion guidance for particular substances under active regulatory review (NAC being a recent, prominent example), signaling that the agency can pause enforcement on a specific ingredient category while broader policy questions are resolved — without that pause amounting to a permanent green light.

How FDA is actually enforcing this in 2026

The agency is reading your marketing, not just your label.

2026 enforcement activity has been unusually active and specific. FDA has issued warning letters over supplement claims identified directly from company websites and social media — not just physical labels — and in at least one case cited a company executive's statements made in a podcast interview as promotional claims that violated labeling rules. Joint FDA/FTC warning letter campaigns have specifically targeted supplements claiming to treat diabetes, infertility, and depression, and FDA has issued a dozen letters over supplement products making prostate-health, erectile-dysfunction, and urinary-health claims — several including anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory claims that pushed well past what a structure-function claim can say.

Separately, cGMP (current Good Manufacturing Practice) violations under 21 CFR Part 111 remain a live enforcement track independent of claims issues entirely — inadequate product specifications, insufficient identity/purity testing methods, and incomplete documentation of packaging and labeling operations have each been cited in 2026 warning letters as grounds for a product being adulterated.

Verdict

If your compliance review only checks the physical label, it's checking half the surface FDA is actually reviewing. Marketing copy anywhere public — a landing page, an Instagram caption, a founder's interview — is fair game for the same scrutiny as the bottle itself.

What it costs to get it wrong
What actually happens when it's missed
Unreported structure-function claim — failure to notify FDA within 30 days of first marketing the claim is itself a compliance failure, independent of whether the claim's substance is otherwise fine.21 U.S.C. §343(r)(6)
Undisclosed disease claim — the product is treated as an unapproved new drug, triggering misbranding under FDCA §502(a) and §502(f)(1), with FDA authorized to pursue warning letters, seizure, or injunction.FDCA §301(a), §502; 21 U.S.C. 331(a), 352
Undisclosed NDI — marketing a product with a new dietary ingredient lacking adequate safety notification renders it adulterated under 21 U.S.C. 342(f), independent of any labeling issue.21 U.S.C. 342(f), 350b
cGMP violations — inadequate manufacturing, testing, or documentation practices under 21 CFR Part 111 can independently render a product adulterated, triggering warning letters even where every claim on the label is compliant.21 CFR Part 111
