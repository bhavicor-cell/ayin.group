When Two Countries Both
Think You Live There.

A treaty — not a feeling, and not a preference — decides. How Article 4 tie-breaker tests actually work, in the order they're applied, and what you need to prove at each step.

In thirty seconds
The plain version
  • Each country sets its own domestic test for tax residency — day counts, permanent homes, economic ties. When two countries' domestic tests both claim you in the same year, you're a dual resident, and both could tax your worldwide income unless a treaty resolves it.
  • Most bilateral tax treaties follow the OECD Model Convention, Article 4(2), which resolves dual residency through a strict, sequential ladder of tests — you work through them in order and stop the moment one produces a clear answer.
  • Being a treaty resident of one country doesn't change your domestic status in the other — a US company remains a domestic corporation regardless of its treaty residence; a dual-resident individual generally must actively notify the tax authority they're relying on the treaty position, not just assume it applies.
  • Claiming treaty residency doesn't automatically exempt you from every domestic obligation — a recent Korean Supreme Court case found that asserting treaty tie-breaker residency did not, on its own, exempt a taxpayer from foreign-account reporting duties for years before a specific legislative exemption took effect.
The tie-breaker ladder

A sequence, not a menu — stop at the first test that resolves it.

1Permanent home

Is there a dwelling available for your continuous use in one country, or both? Any form of dwelling qualifies — owned, rented, or otherwise occupied — but a hotel room or short-term rental lacking the indicia of permanence doesn't count. If a permanent home exists in only one country, that settles it. If it exists in both — or in neither — move to the next test.

2Center of vital interests

Where are your personal and economic ties actually strongest? Spouse and children, primary employment or business oversight, main bank accounts and investment management, club and community memberships, even where you're registered to vote. This is a comparative weighing exercise, not a checklist with a pass mark.

3Habitual abode

If neither prior test resolves it, this asks where you actually have the habit of living — examined through the pattern and frequency of your presence over time, not a single year's day count in isolation.

4Nationality

If habitual abode still doesn't resolve it — you have one in both countries, or in neither — residency falls to whichever country you're a national of.

5Mutual agreement

If you're a national of both countries, or of neither, the two tax authorities must resolve it directly through a competent-authority procedure — a formal negotiation between the governments themselves, with no guaranteed timeline.

Worked example

A French national owns a house in France, where her spouse and children live, her primary bank accounts sit, and her driver's license is registered. She also rents a condo in California and spends eight months of the year there for a work project — long enough to cross the US Substantial Presence Test and become a US tax resident under domestic law. France separately treats her as a resident because her family and main home are there.

She's now a dual resident. Step one — permanent home — doesn't resolve it; she has one in both countries (the French house, the rented California condo). Step two — center of vital interests — does: her spouse, children, primary financial accounts, and legal registration are all clearly in France. The tie-breaker assigns her treaty residence to France, even though she physically spent more of the year in California.

Verdict

Physical presence and day counts matter enormously for triggering domestic residency tests in the first place — but once you're a genuine dual resident, the tie-breaker doesn't simply hand the win to whichever country you spent more days in. Center of vital interests can override a pure presence count.

Treaty residence doesn't erase domestic status

Winning the tie-breaker changes tax liability — not your legal classification at home.

A common misreading: assuming that a favorable tie-breaker result makes the "losing" country's domestic claim disappear entirely. It doesn't. A corporation that's a dual resident of the US and a treaty jurisdiction remains a domestic US corporation regardless of where its treaty residence lands — the tie-breaker only determines which country has primary taxing rights under the treaty, not the entity's underlying legal status.

For individuals, the same caution applies in reverse: dual residents who want to compute their US tax liability as if they were nonresident aliens, based on a treaty tie-breaker favoring the other country, generally must proactively notify the US tax authority that this is the position they're taking. Fail to notify, and the default is continued treatment as a US resident for tax computation purposes — the treaty position doesn't apply itself.

The trap

A recent case from Korea's tax authority made the same point from the other direction: a taxpayer who simply asserted dual-residency status under a treaty tie-breaker, without more, was found by the Supreme Court not to be automatically exempt from a separate domestic reporting obligation for foreign financial accounts — for years before a specific legislative exemption was enacted. Treaty residence and domestic compliance obligations are related but distinct questions; winning one doesn't automatically resolve the other unless the law says so explicitly.

Proving it

A tax residency certificate is the document that makes the claim usable.

Once you know where you're actually resident, you typically need to prove it — to claim treaty benefits, reduce withholding tax on cross-border dividends, interest, or royalties, and to satisfy banks and counterparties under FATCA and CRS reporting regimes. A Tax Residency Certificate (TRC), issued by your home tax authority, is the standard document for this. In the US, this means filing Form 8802 and paying the applicable user fee, after which the IRS issues Form 6166 as the certificate itself.

  • Employers, take note — a senior remote employee with authority to bind the company can create a permanent establishment for the employer in the employee's country of tax residency, dragging corporate income tax into a jurisdiction the company never planned to operate in. This risk sits entirely separate from the employee's own personal tie-breaker analysis.
