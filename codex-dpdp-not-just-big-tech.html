The DPDP Act Isn't
Just for Big Tech.

If you have a signup form, a customer list, or an app with Indian users — this is already your law, not someone else's.

JurisdictionIndia (extraterritorial)
VerticalAny business collecting digital personal data

In thirty seconds
The plain version
  • The Act applies to digital personal data — a lower bar than most assume. A name and phone number in your CRM counts. So does an email list.
  • It's extraterritorial. A foreign entity offering goods or services to individuals in India — or processing Indian residents' data in connection with that offering — is in scope regardless of where the company sits.
  • The rules were notified 13 November 2025, rolling out across three phases ending with full enforcement on 13 May 2027.
  • Penalties are flat rupee ceilings, not percentage-of-turnover — up to ₹250 crore for security failures, up to ₹200 crore for consent or breach-notification failures — and they run alongside a separate civil right to compensation.
Scope

If you have a signup form, a customer database, or an app — stop reading this as someone else's problem.

The Act applies to digital personal data — data about an identifiable individual, collected or processed in digital form. That's a lower bar than most people assume. It's not just biometric data or financial records: a customer's name and phone number in your CRM, an email list, a booking form — all of it counts.

It also reaches further than your own borders. The Act applies extraterritorially — if you're a foreign entity offering goods or services to individuals in India, or processing Indian residents' data in connection with that offering, you're in scope regardless of where your company sits. A startup with entirely non-Indian founders but Indian users is still a Data Fiduciary under this law.

Verdict

If you have a signup form, a booking system, an email list, or an app with Indian users, you are almost certainly already a Data Fiduciary. Check your actual data flows before assuming otherwise.

The timeline

Three dates, and where you actually stand right now.

Phase 1Already in force

The Data Protection Board of India is established and operational. Digital complaint filing has already begun. If you haven't started thinking about this, you're already behind the awareness curve, even if enforcement hasn't reached you yet.

Phase 213 Nov 2026

The Consent Manager framework goes live — registered third-party intermediaries through which individuals manage, review, and withdraw consent across services. If your business relies on consent as a legal basis (most ordinary businesses do), your systems need to be technically compatible.

Phase 313 May 2027

The hard deadline. Full substantive compliance — notice, consent, breach notification, data principal rights, retention and deletion — becomes enforceable. No grace period after this date.

Verdict

2026 is the build year, not the panic year — but it's not a free year either. The Board has already initiated enforcement action against companies processing data without valid consent. "Soft enforcement" does not mean "no enforcement."

Valid consent

What counts — and what doesn't.

Valid consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous — given through a clear affirmative action, granular per purpose.

FreeNo coercion
SpecificPurpose-bound
InformedPlainly explained
UnconditionalNot tied to service
UnambiguousAffirmative act

Here's what that rules out — and it rules out things extremely common in Indian business practice today: pre-ticked checkboxes (not valid — the individual has to actively check it), bundled consent (marketing, data sharing, and core terms in one blanket "I agree" — not valid; consent has to be granular, purpose by purpose), and consent inferred from continued use ("by continuing to use this site you agree" — not valid, if this is actually your legal basis). Withdrawal has to be exactly as easy as giving consent — one-click signup demands one-click unsubscribe, not a support ticket.

Verdict

Audit your actual signup, checkout, and onboarding flows against this list before anything else. This is the single most common gap — and it's fixable without an engineering overhaul in most cases. It's a forms-and-copy problem, not always a systems problem.

Notice

A separate document, not a clause buried in your Terms of Service.

The Act requires standalone privacy notices — clear, itemized explanations of what data you collect and why, separate from your general terms of service. If your only privacy disclosure today is a paragraph inside a long Terms and Conditions page nobody reads, that's not what the law asks for. Where consent is the legal basis, that notice also needs a dedicated, easy mechanism to withdraw consent, exercise rights, or file a grievance.

Data Principal rights

The ones your business actually needs to operationalize.

  • Access — what personal data you hold about them.
  • Correction and erasure — including automated deletion once the purpose is served, not indefinite retention "just in case."
  • Nomination — someone who can exercise these rights on their behalf after death or incapacity.
  • Grievance redressal — a named Grievance Officer, with contact details published somewhere findable.

Practical build for an ordinary business: a dedicated rights-request channel (a privacy email address is enough for most small operations), an identity-verification step, a response process within the Rules' timelines, and a log of every request kept for at least three years.

Breach notification

The overlap almost nobody flags.

If a breach occurs, you must notify the Data Protection Board and affected individuals promptly, with a detailed follow-up report within 72 hours. But CERT-In's existing 2022 direction already requires reporting of specified cyber incidents within six hours — a separate regulator, a separate clock, a separate channel. The same breach can trigger both obligations simultaneously, on different timelines.

Verdict

Build one incident response playbook that satisfies both regimes at once — don't discover the overlap mid-breach.

Significant Data Fiduciary

Probably not you — but check anyway.

The government can designate certain entities — based on data volume, sensitivity, and risk — as Significant Data Fiduciaries, carrying extra obligations: an India-based Data Protection Officer, annual impact assessments, data-localization scrutiny, and algorithmic due diligence. Expected to catch large fintechs, telecoms, major e-commerce, and big social platforms. Classification isn't self-declared; the government notifies it.

Verdict

Most readers of this piece are Data Fiduciaries, not Significant Data Fiduciaries — but "probably not" is not the same as "confirmed not," and growth can change your classification without you initiating anything.

What it costs to get it wrong
Flat rupee ceilings — not percentage of turnover
Failing to implement reasonable security safeguards — up to ₹250 crore.Section 33, weighed against breach severity, intent, cooperation
Failing to notify a breach, processing without valid consent, or failing to protect children's data — up to ₹200 crore.Section 33

These run alongside — not instead of — an individual's separate right to seek compensation through civil courts. Both routes are available at the same time. These are ceilings, not automatic amounts, but they're large enough that "we're too small to matter" isn't a safe assumption once Phase 3 enforcement begins in earnest.

What to actually do

In order.

01

Confirm you're actually in scope — check your real data flows, not your assumptions. If you collect any identifiable data digitally from anyone in India, start here.

02

Audit your consent flows against the five-part test, fixing pre-ticked boxes and bundled consent first — the cheapest, fastest corrections available.

03

Separate your privacy notice from your Terms of Service, itemized and specific, not a general clause.

04

Build the operational rights infrastructure — a request channel, identity verification, a response timeline, a log, a named Grievance Officer.

05

Write one incident response plan that satisfies both DPDP's 72-hour report and CERT-In's six-hour window, before you need it for real.

06

Calendar the two hard dates — November 2026 for Consent Manager compatibility, May 2027 for full compliance — and work backward from them now.
