RoHS & WEEE:
Two Rules, One Product.

Substance restrictions and producer take-back obligations that travel with every shipment — and are governed by two entirely different mechanisms most sellers assume are one.

JurisdictionEU (27 states) + UK, separate national registers
VerticalElectronics & electrical equipment
StageMap → Clear
Last reviewed15 Jul 2026
In thirty seconds
The plain version
  • RoHS (Directive 2011/65/EU) restricts what's inside the product — ten hazardous substances capped by weight, checked once at design and manufacturing.
  • WEEE (Directive 2012/19/EU) governs what happens after the product is discarded — producer registration, take-back financing, and recycling, checked continuously for as long as you sell.
  • They're both directives, not regulations — meaning each of the 27 EU member states implements its own national scheme, register, and enforcement body. There is no single EU-wide registration for either.
  • Since 2018, essentially all electrical and electronic equipment falls under WEEE's scope — the old ten-category list was opened up, so "my product type isn't listed" is rarely a real exemption anymore.
Two regimes, constantly confused
Substance restriction RoHS

Checked once, at design and manufacturing. Restricts ten substances by weight in homogeneous materials. Compliance is a technical file and a declaration of conformity — a design question, not an ongoing operational one.

Producer responsibility WEEE

Ongoing, for as long as you place products on the market. Requires registration in every country you sell into, regular reporting of volumes placed, and financing of collection and recycling — an operational and financial commitment, not a one-time design check.

Verdict

Being RoHS-compliant says nothing about your WEEE status, and vice versa. Sellers frequently discover this the hard way — a product with a clean RoHS technical file gets flagged or delisted for having no WEEE producer registration in the country it's being sold into.

RoHS — what's restricted

Ten substances, capped by weight, in every homogeneous material.

Lead0.1%
Mercury0.1%
Cadmium0.01%
Hex. chromium0.1%
PBB0.1%
PBDE0.1%
DEHP0.1%
BBP0.1%
DBP0.1%
DIBP0.1%

The first six substances were restricted from the original 2011 recast; the four phthalates (DEHP, BBP, DBP, DIBP) were added by Directive 2015/863 and have applied since 22 July 2019 for most equipment, with medical devices and monitoring/control instruments given until 2021. Thresholds are 0.1% by weight per homogeneous material for every substance except cadmium, capped at 0.01%.

Exemptions exist where substitution isn't yet technically or scientifically feasible — lead in certain solders, glass, ceramics, and high-reliability alloys, for instance — but each exemption is narrow, tied to a specific application and product category, and carries its own expiry date. The Commission reviews Annex III exemptions roughly every five years, and 2026 has brought a wave of narrowed lead exemptions specifically, with several split into more specific sub-entries and shorter validity windows rather than outright removal.

The trap

A RoHS exemption is not the same as being outside RoHS's scope. If your product is in scope, a specific material or component may still qualify for an exemption — but only if it matches the exact wording, category, and concentration limit in the Annex, and only until that exemption's expiry date. Relying on an exemption that's expired, or that doesn't match your exact application, is treated the same as having no exemption at all.

WEEE — what it actually requires

Registration, reporting, and financing — repeated in every country you sell into.

If you manufacture, import, or sell electrical or electronic equipment into an EU member state — including as a marketplace seller using a fulfilment service — you are classified as a producer under WEEE. That triggers three ongoing obligations, in every country where you place products:

  • Register with the national WEEE authority — Stiftung EAR in Germany, the CdC RAEE registry in Italy, and equivalent bodies elsewhere. There is no single EU-wide register; registering in one country does not cover another.
  • Report regularly the types and volumes of equipment placed on that market, in the format each national register specifies.
  • Finance take-back and recycling — usually by joining a Producer Responsibility Organisation (PRO), which handles collection and treatment on your behalf for a fee tied to product category and weight. Setting up your own take-back system is technically possible but rarely practical given the national coverage it requires.

Non-EU sellers with no established presence in a given member state must appoint a WEEE Authorised Representative in that country. Germany's national law, for instance, explicitly requires this for any company without a German establishment selling into that market.

Verdict

WEEE compliance is not a single action — it's a standing operational relationship with a PRO (or a representative) in every market you sell into, renewed and reported on an ongoing basis, not a box checked once at launch.

What's changing

Both regimes are under active revision in 2026.

The European Commission published its evaluation of the WEEE Directive on 2 July 2025, identifying persistent shortcomings: a collection rate stuck around 40% of e-waste actually collected and recycled, fragmented national EPR schemes, inconsistent treatment standards, and enforcement gaps specifically around online and distance sellers. A formal revision proposal is expected via the broader EU Circular Economy Act, potentially converting WEEE from a directive into a directly applicable regulation — which would replace 27 separate national schemes with one harmonised system, similar in spirit to how GPSR replaced the old product safety directive.

On the RoHS side, no new substances have been added to the restricted list as of mid-2026, but the Commission has been actively tightening existing exemptions — particularly lead-related ones — through delegated directives, most recently a draft published 8 July 2026 proposing further revisions to Annexes III and IV, open for feedback until early August 2026.

Verdict

Don't build compliance processes around today's fragmented WEEE landscape as if it's permanent — a shift toward a single EU-wide regulation is realistically on the horizon, and RoHS exemptions your product currently relies on should be checked against their specific expiry dates, not assumed to renew automatically.

What it costs to get it wrong
Two regimes, two different failure modes
RoHS non-compliance — a product containing a restricted substance above threshold, with no valid exemption, cannot legally bear CE marking and cannot be placed on the EU market. Market surveillance authorities can order withdrawal or recall; national penalties vary by member state.Directive 2011/65/EU, Art. 4
No WEEE registration in a given country — the product is non-compliant in that specific market even if it's registered elsewhere; national authorities and marketplaces both act on this independently, and marketplace delisting is typically the faster consequence.Directive 2012/19/EU, national implementing law
Marketplace enforcement — platforms increasingly require WEEE registration numbers and RoHS documentation directly in seller onboarding; missing either can trigger listing suspension independent of any regulator taking action.Practitioner reporting, national WEEE registers
