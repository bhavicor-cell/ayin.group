Krutarth E. Founder — Ayin & ORR A five-minute read No résumé follows

From an early age, I questioned almost everything. Literally anything.

Read the account
IThe question

Two things I could never stand:

01 information asymmetry
02 and people being exploited because of it.

I also disliked accepting something simply because someone — or something — said it was true.

Looking back, almost everything I have built has been, in one way or another, a response to those two things.

IIThe thread
01Chemistry

My curiosity about understanding what sits beneath the surface eventually led me to study chemistry. To me, chemistry wasn't just another subject — it felt like the foundation of almost everything.

02Environment · Canada

While studying chemistry, one area always pulled me in more than the others: the environment. I worked on environmental projects, presented them nationally and internationally, and eventually decided to pursue Environmental Management and Assessment in Canada. I wanted to understand what had actually led us to where we are today, rather than simply discussing the consequences.

03Law & public policy

During that degree, I found myself becoming increasingly interested in law and public policy. I enjoyed researching them, writing policy papers, and understanding not just what policies said, but why they existed in the first place.

04Regulatory affairs

That naturally led me into Regulatory Affairs, where I began looking at regulation through a policy and legal lens.

05The floor

Interestingly, while I was learning how policies were designed and what they intended to achieve, I was also working on the floor where those same policies had to function.

From perishables and baby products to electronics, I worked inside large multinational companies and watched how internal policies translated into everyday operations.

IIIThe gap

The gap was obvious.

Not because policymakers failed to understand reality — they usually do. They simply have to work within practical limits, competing priorities, politics, economics, and countless other considerations.

But standing on the operational side, I could see how often policies became difficult to execute because the infrastructure wasn't there, the processes weren't realistic, or the organisation simply wasn't built to support them.

Over nearly five years, I experienced policy from almost every angle I could.

I studied it. I researched it. I wrote about it. And then I watched people try to live with it.
IVORR

That eventually led me to start ORR.

Originally, it began in Canada through personal connections and freelance work with local businesses, helping solve governance and operational problems.

As things started gaining momentum, life changed unexpectedly.

An illness nearly gave me a second life.

It forced me to step back, rethink where I wanted to build, and eventually brought me back to India, where ORR became a proper firm rather than simply freelance work.

Since then, I've had the privilege of working with national and international clients, including several long-term partnerships.

VThe pattern

Across different industries, I kept noticing something that refused to go away.

Governance failures were being treated as operational failures.
Operational failures were being treated as team failures.
Team failures were being treated as individual failures.
The symptoms changed. The underlying problem usually didn't.

At times, it honestly felt ridiculous.

How could organisations this large operate like this?

But over time I also understood something important.

Founders almost have to develop tunnel vision. Without it, they never build anything. Growth demands focus, and that focus often leaves little room for thinking about governance until tomorrow suddenly becomes today.

For the last several years, that has been the work I've enjoyed most — helping founders and organisations untangle those problems before they become something much larger.

VIThe order

Working both inside organisations and alongside them taught me another lesson.

Very few companies think seriously about compliance early. Which is understandable. Regulatory action feels like a possibility. Growth feels immediate. Naturally, growth wins.

But compliance isn't simply about avoiding notices, penalties, or regulatory action. It quietly shapes how an organisation builds itself.

Most companies build something like this:

01Vision.
02Product.
03Team.
04Operations.
05Revenue.
06Compliance.

To me, that order always felt backwards.

Compliance shouldn't be the final layer added to an already-built business. It should quietly influence how the business is built from the very beginning — not because regulators expect it, but because rebuilding foundations is always harder than building them properly the first time.

Around the same time, I continued studying law through my LLB. To me, it never felt like changing direction. It felt like continuing the same journey through another lens.

Chemistry→ Environment→ Policy→ Regulatory Affairs→ Operations→ Law

Eventually, everything
came together in Ayin.

An International Advisory and Compliance firm built around helping founders and businesses navigate international markets, regulatory complexity, and market access that often feels far more intimidating than it actually is.

VIIIAsked, still
Asked — often

People sometimes ask whether I still dislike information asymmetry.

Absolutely. Probably even more than I used to.

We're surrounded by experts — or at least people calling themselves experts. Many are genuinely knowledgeable. Some aren't.

I don't dislike expertise. I dislike unnecessary obscurity.

But wherever unnecessary information asymmetry can be reduced, I believe it should be.

That's why Ayin has the Codex. That's why ORR publishes its systems. They're public because I believe people make better decisions when they actually understand what they're dealing with.

If someone learns something useful without ever becoming a client, I'm perfectly comfortable with that.

Asked — still

People also ask whether I still care about environmental issues.

I do. I probably always will.

I've simply become more realistic about what one person can change. I still spend time outdoors. I still follow developments in environmental law and policy. I still participate in discussions where I feel I can contribute something meaningful. I just no longer feel the need to fight every battle.

Some things are bigger than any individual. That doesn't mean they're not worth caring about.

If you've read this far, thank you.

Whether we ever work together or not, I appreciate that you've taken the time to understand how I think before deciding what I do.

Ayin wasn't built to sell compliance. It was built because I believe businesses deserve to understand the systems they operate within — not simply react to them when something goes wrong.

We are not perfect. And we can't do everything by ourselves.

If you have a problem you think I might be able to help with, I'd be glad to have a conversation.
