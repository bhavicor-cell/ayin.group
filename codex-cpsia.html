Isn't Optional.

Not once a product is marketed to a child. What CPSIA actually requires, how the CPSC decides if your product counts, and the new electronic filing mandate arriving this year.

In thirty seconds
The plain version
  • CPSIA (2008) covers any product "primarily designed or intended for children 12 years of age or younger." That's a marketing-and-intent test, not a strict product-category list — which is exactly where sellers get it wrong.
  • Products subject to a children's product safety rule need third-party testing by a CPSC-accredited lab, and a Children's Product Certificate (CPC) based on that testing, before the product ships.
  • A "material change" to a certified product — a new zipper, new ink, a new supplier for the same part — can trigger full retesting and recertification. Compliance isn't a one-time event; it tracks the product's actual bill of materials.
  • Starting 8 July 2026, most CPSC-regulated imports require electronic filing of certificate data directly to U.S. Customs and Border Protection before the shipment can clear — a structural shift from paper-based spot checks to review at the border, before goods even land.
Classification

The four-factor test — and the misclassification trap.

The CPSC weighs four factors to decide whether something counts as a children's product:

Manufacturer's statementWhat the maker says the product is intended for.
Marketing & packagingHow it's presented, described, and displayed at retail.
Common recognitionWhether a reasonable consumer would see it as meant for children.
Age grading guidelinesEstablished industry norms for what age uses the product.

This is where a lot of otherwise careful compliance programs still slip. A garment sold as "daywear" or "loungewear" but actually intended for sleep gets reclassified by the CPSC as children's sleepwear — which triggers separate flammability rules under 16 CFR Parts 1615 and 1616 that a plain daywear listing wouldn't need. Companies have faced recalls and enforcement specifically because packaging language implied a use case that pulled in requirements the product was never tested against.

Verdict

Check your own marketing copy and packaging language against these four factors before assuming your product sits outside CPSIA's scope — the test looks at what a reasonable buyer would conclude, not just your internal product category label.

What compliance actually requires

Testing, certification, and the paper trail that has to survive a material change.

  • Third-party testing — a CPSC-accepted accredited laboratory must test the finished product against every applicable children's product safety rule. For toys specifically, this includes ASTM F963 testing. Testing must happen before shipment; relying on a supplier's own testing, or assuming a comparable product's results apply to yours, is not a substitute.
  • Children's Product Certificate (CPC) — issued by the manufacturer or importer, based on and referencing the accredited lab's test report. No valid test report means no valid CPC, and the certificate must accompany every shipment, available to distributors, retailers, and customs on request.
  • Periodic testing — for products in continuous production, retesting is required at least once a year after initial certification, following a documented Periodic Testing Plan.
  • Material change retesting — under 16 CFR Part 1107, a change in design, materials, or manufacturing process can require full retesting and a new certificate. This has real teeth: a supplier switching the zipper, ink, or a single component on an already-certified product can invalidate the existing CPC without anyone updating the paperwork.
  • Lead limits — CPSIA dropped the lead limit in surface coatings from 600 ppm to 90 ppm, and current substrate limits sit at 100 ppm total lead content, phased in over several years after the 2008 Act. Phthalates in toys and child-care articles are separately capped at 0.1% under Section 108.
The 2026 change

eFiling moves the check to the border, before goods land.

8 Jul 2026Most imports

Electronic filing of certificate of compliance data becomes mandatory for most CPSC-regulated imported products, submitted directly to U.S. Customs and Border Protection via a Partner Government Agency Message Set.

8 Jan 2027FTZ imports

The eFiling requirement extends to products imported through Foreign Trade Zones.

Verdict

Under the old paper-based system, incomplete or vague certificate data was often a downstream problem, caught after the fact. Under eFiling, missing or inaccurate elements — vague product descriptions, incomplete rule citations — can flag or delay a shipment before it even reaches U.S. shores. A certificate that was "good enough" for a paper file may not survive automated review. This is the single most consequential near-term change for anyone currently importing children's products into the US.
